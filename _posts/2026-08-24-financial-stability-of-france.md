### 1. Macroeconomic indicators and aggregates (2010–2024)

1.1. Institutional context: Dirigisme and the neo-Keynesian model

Understanding the financial position of France starts from the analysis of the fundamen
tal institutional characteristics that make it unique among developed economies, known in the
academic literature as the phenomenon of dirigisme. This term, which has its roots in post-wa
r reconstruction and the planned economy of the De Gaulle period, describes active state inter
vention in the coordination of economic activities, industrial policy and financial markets (Hal
l and Soskice, 2001; Schmidt, 2002). France did not accept a purely market, Anglo-Saxon mo
del of capitalism, but historically developed its own hybrid form in which public administratio
n plays a crucial role in resource allocation, credit expansion and strategic investments. This i
nstitutional architecture has direct implications for macroeconomic aggregates. High levels of
public expenditures, which in the period from 2010 to 2024 consistently exceeded 55% of GD
P, reflect this model of the welfare state (État-providence), and Paris, it seems, does not want t
o compromise that even in conditions of fiscal pressure. The public debt of France exceeded th
e threshold of 90% of GDP back in 2012, and reached some 115% of GDP in 2020, due to tho
se COVID-19 fiscal interventions. It stabilized later at around 112% of GDP by 2024. That ti
me is, at the same time, with the deadline and the consequence of the negative NIIP; the state
constantly borrows on the international capital markets, and this increases the foreign share in
the total financial claims against France.

1.2. GDP movement and structural dynamics

Key macroeconomic data for France are presented in Table 1. The data comes from the
IMF's World Economic Outlook (April 2025), from the Banque de France and from the EWN
database. Gross domestic product, the main indicator of economic activity, grew from USD 2,
648 billion in 2010 to USD 3,161 billion in 2024. The increase was accompanied by large fluc
tuations as the EUR/USD exchange rate changed. Gross national income (GNI) remains close
to nominal GDP, and net factor income is positive because it comes from direct investments a
broad. Real GDP growth is moderate. The average is about 1.5 % per year in the period 2010
–2019. One exception is a fall of -8.0 % in 2020 due to COVID-19, while the recovery is +6.8
% in 2021. 

Inflation (consumer price index / harmonized consumer price index) was low and stabl
e throughout the period 2010–2019. (an average of 1.1% per year according to the Banque de
France). This is in line with the ECB's target of 2%. But the energy crisis and post-Covid prob
lems in supply chains have raised inflation. Inflation jumped to 5.9% in 2022 and 5.7% in 202
3 – the highest since the oil crises of the 1970s – and the ECB responded with its sharpest rate
hike ever. The unemployment rate was between 7.3% and 10.4%. The unemployment rate reac
hed its highest level in the eurozone crisis from 2012 to 2015. The unemployment rate then gr
adually fell and reached 7.3 % in 2024 – the unemployment rate was then close to the lowest
point from 2008 , according to Eurostat.

Investments (gross investments in fixed capital) remained stable. Investments amounte
d to 22–24 % of GDP throughout the entire period. This is slightly below the average of the G
7 group. The IMF says household investment fell 6 % in 2024 and 8.2 % in 2023. High interes
t rates caused the decline, especially in housing construction and consumer lending. Total con
sumption (private + public) still accounts for more than 80 % of GDP. In 2020, public consum
ption grew by 2.1 % and showed a countercyclical role. In 2024, exports record further growth
to USD 1,004 billion, while imports slightly stabilize at USD 1,001 billion, resulting in a sligh
t trade surplus – this puts France in fourth place in terms of exports in Europe. The indicator o
f financial globalization (A+L)/gross domestic product increased from 5.85 in 2010 to 7.97 in
2020 and settled down to 7.25 in 2024. This shows that France is the strongest financial econo
my of the eurozone in terms of external integration.

1.3. Credit expansion and Credit-to-GDP paradox

One of the most striking things about the structure of the French economy is how muc
h it relies on credit, if we look at the total amount of credit in relation to GDP. Data from Ban
que de France and BIS show that the total amount of credit (including both private and public
sector) is constantly above 200-250% of GDP. By itself, that number doesn't necessarily mea
n there will be problems - Japan has an even higher credit-to-GDP ratio - but when combined
with a negative NIIP and rising public debt, it suggests that the system is dependent on externa
l liabilities being constantly refinanced.The high loan-to-GDP ratio is directly related to how the banking system is set up, whe
re according to data from the European Central Bank (ECB) and umbrella financial statements
, the largest banks BNP Paribas, Crédit Agricole, Société Générale and BPCE together hold
3more than 45-55% of the total assets of the banking sector, and their total balance is many tim
es higher than the annual GDP of France (Ayadi et al., 2010). However, it is important to emp
hasize that only Société Générale and BNP Paribas are primarily joint-stock companies, while
Crédit Agricole and BPCE have their roots in cooperatives. Namely, Crédit Agricole was creat
ed from the network of cooperative banks (Caisses régionales de Crédit Agricole Mutuel), and
BPCE from the merger of Banques Populaires and Caisses d'Épargne. This difference is not o
nly a matter of history; the cooperative model means a different way of thinking, a more cauti
ous approach to risk and a greater focus on long-term stability rather than looking at how to in
crease the value of shares in the short term.

The concept of "banks as national champions" is directly linked to state control. The F
rench state not only regulates, but also actively buys shares and informally protects financial i
nstitutions that are key to the system. In crisis periods (2008–2009, 2011–2012, 2020), the Fre
nch state demonstrated its role through clear state guarantees, capital replenishment and joint a
ctions that prevented a major collapse of the system. The paradox is that the French state provi
des protection that encourages more credit and thus increases the ratio of credit to gross domes
tic product

### 2. Balance of payments and financial flows

2.1. Structural analysis of the current account

The current account of France's balance of payments was permanently in deficit in the
period from 2010 to 2024, which is quite different from the German surplus and creates one of
the major tensions within the eurozone. The biggest minuses were in 2012 (-32.9 billion USD)
, then in 2018 (-20.5 billion USD), and especially in 2020 (-53.0 billion USD). This deteriorat
ion occurred due to COVID-19, which meant fewer tourists (tourism is close to 8% of GDP),
problems with production chains and higher imports of medical equipment and energy sources
(INSEE, 2024). It is important to say that 2019 was the only year in this period (until 2024) w
hen France had a surplus, namely +16.0 billion USD. This happened because the export of ser
vices increased, the situation with energy products improved, and tourism was a record. In 2024, a small plus of +2.7 billion US
D was recorded, which may mean that something is changing, but we cannot say that with cert
ainty yet.

2.2. Financial flows and the role of France in the global network of capital

Seen through the financial account of the balance of payments, it can be seen that France is among the largest countries in the world in terms of capital exports and imports. Total external assets rose from USD 7.58 trillion in 2010 to USD 11.01 trillion in 2024, a growth of 45
%, while total liabilities increased from USD 7.92 to 11.91 trillion in the same period. The difference in the growth of assets and liabilities explains why the NIIP was getting worse during that period. The key flows within the financial account include: (1) direct investments abroad (FDI
outflows), where France shows dominance in European M&A transactions – FDI assets grew from 1.56 to 1.94 trillion USD; (2) portfolio investments that reflect the deep integration of Par
is as a global financial center – portfolio assets (shares + debt) increased from 2.81 to 3.74 trillion USD; and (3) other investments (mainly banking flows) that become unstable due to the volatile behavior of large banks in periods of stress.
The role of financial derivatives is particularly interesting. Assets and liabilities in derivatives were relatively balanced throughout the observed period (the difference is usually belo
w USD 100 billion), which suggests that France uses derivatives mainly for hedging purposes and not as an instrument of speculation. Nevertheless, the absolute numbers themselves (more
than 800 billion USD on each side) indicate exceptionally sophisticated balance sheets of individual institutions (ECB, 2024)

2.3. Implications for external vulnerability

The combination of a deficit current account and an expansive financial account creates a specific type of vulnerability for France: the country is not directly exposed to the risk of a sudden stop of capital flows (eng. sudden s top), to the extent that these are less developed eco
nomies, because the euro is the world's reserve currency and because the Paris capital market is one of the deepest in the world. However, the accumulation of negative NIIP through multi-year current account deficits inevitably leads to an increase in net transfer payments to foreign
creditors, which consequently reduces the available income for domestic consumption as well as investments.

### 3. International Net Investment Position (NIIP)

3.1 Conceptual framework and methodological notes

The Net International Investment Position (NIIP) - or as it is written in the IMF's Balan
ce of Payments and International Investment Position Manual (BPM6) - shows the difference
between all external financial claims of a country and all financial liabilities towards foreign r
esidents. NIIP is not just an accounting item. According to the theoretical framework of profe
ssor Čaušević (2020), NIIP shows how economically strong the country is and how sovereign
it is. A positive NIIP means that the country is entitled to more resources than the world. A ne
gative NIIP means that foreign actors have claims against that country.In this paper, we use the EWN database. The database offers two versions of the NIIP.
(1) NIIP without gold reserves ("Net IIP excl. gold") covers all financial flows, except moneta
ry gold. (2) Official NIIP ("Official IIP") includes monetary gold at market prices. The differ
ence between these two measures shows the role of gold reserves in the balance of the external
sector.

3.2 NIIP Trends: Systemic Deterioration 2010–2024

Analysis of data from EWN shows a worrying trend. NIIP excluding gold reserves fell
from -336.96 USD (that's -12.6 % of GDP) in 2010 to -1,156.96 USD (about -37.8 % of GDP)
in 2023. NIIP without gold reserves temporarily improved to - USD 901.10 billion (-29.7 % of
GDP) in 2024. This means a drop of almost a trillion dollars in NIIP without gold reserves in
14 years. This is one of the biggest changes in the external financial position of a developed ec
onomy in that period. The worst deteriorations occurred in two phases: (1) 2012–2013. NIIP/GDP fell from
-17.5% to -22.6%. This is a direct consequence of the eurozone debt crisis and the large finan
cial intermediation of French banks in covering the debts of peripheral countries; and (2) 201
7–2021. NIIP/GDP fell from -29.3% to -36.9%. This is the joint effect of the growth of public
debt, fiscal measures due to COVID-19 and the large expansion of bank balance sheets under t
he ECB's ultra-low interest rates

3.3 Difference between Official NIIP and NIIP without gold reserves: Analytical significance

The difference between the two NIIP measures moved from $83 billion to $204 billion
over the period. The NIIP difference showed how gold prices change and how much gold the
Banque de France holds. The NIIP difference helps us understand France better.
France has one of the largest gold reserves in the world – the (World Gold Council) sa
ys the Banque de France holds about 2,437 metric tons of gold coins. The third largest gold re
serve is France, after the USA and Germany. At the end of 2024, the gold reserve is worth alm
ost 204 billion US dollars. The highest value in the observed period is 204 billions of US dollars. In 2010, the gold reserve value was only US$110 billion because gold p
rices were lower.
Gold has three roles in the NIIP analysis: (1) Gold is a reserve asset and raises the offic
ial NIIP relative to the NIIP without gold; (2) Gold is the world's safety cushion in crisis scena
rios. Gold cannot be stored in the logic of financial claims because there is no partner who ca
n declare a default on the gold reserve; and (3) Gold is a tool of central bank confidence and s
trengthens market confidence in the stability of the euro and the French financial system.
A paradox is worth mentioning: in the years when gold prices rise (2020–2024), the dif
ference between the official NIIP and the NIIP without gold increases and hides a worse finan
cial situation. In 2020, the NIIP without gold was -US$1,067.68 billion, the official NIIP was
"just" -US$919.29 billion - a difference of US$148 billion due to the estimation of gold reserv
es alone. This statistical artifact has direct implications for regulatory analyses: analysts follo
wing only the official NIIP may systematically underestimate France's external vulnerability.

3.4 Detailed NIIP analysis by asset and liability components

Analysis of the Lane & Milesi-Ferretti components reveals that the largest foreign own
ers are foreign portfolio investors - funds, pension funds and central banks. These investors ho
ld French government bonds (OAT – Obligations Assimilables du Trésor). Data from Agence
France Trésor show that foreign residents hold around 45–55 % of total central sector debt. In
addition, direct foreign investments (FDI liabilities) amounted to 1.014 trillion USD in 2010. I
n 2024, at 1.315 trillion USD, you can see how much foreigners control the profitable parts of
the French economy. Understanding the composition of assets and liabilities is crucial because
different components carry different levels of risk, liquidity and reversibility. The EWN datab
ase divides the external position into five components: direct investments (FDI), portfolio inve
stments in equity, portfolio investments in debt instruments, other investments (mainly bank d
eposits and loans), and financial derivatives. On the asset side, reserve assets (foreign exchan
ge reserves without gold) are added to this.
On the asset side, total external assets grew from $7.582 trillion in 2010 to $11.005 tril
lion in 2024 – a 45% increase. FDI assets, which reflect the ownership share and control of Fr
ench companies in foreign entities, grew from 1.557 to 2.084 billion USD and is relatively sta
ble throughout the entire period because direct investments are by nature long-term and less v
olatile. Behind it are multinational giants such as Total Energies, LVMH, AXA and Airbus, w
hich hold significant stakes in global companies. The portfolio of bond assets – investments in
foreign debt instruments – dominated in absolute terms in 2010 ($2.140 trillion) and remained
at a similar level until 2024 ($2.275 trillion), with fluctuations. This moderate dynamic reflect
s the more conservative policy of institutional investors (insurance companies, pension funds)
who prefer bonds over stocks. Portfolio equity assets, in contrast, showed strong growth – fro
m 665.7 billion to 1.316 billion USD in 2024 – with characteristic oscillations: in 2021 it jump
ed by 21% in one year thanks to the global growth of the stock market, and in 2022 it fell due t
o the restrictive monetary policy of the ECB. Other investments in assets (primarily bank loan
s and deposits) grew from 2.060 to 3.451 trillion USD and became over time the largest item o
f assets. This item is primarily a reflection of cross-border balance sheet expansions of large b
anks, especially during 2020-2024. in the context of the ECB's TLTRO-III program, which en
couraged expansive credit behavior. Financial derivatives on assets increased from 1.103 to 1.
800 trillion USD – but they follow a parallel growth on the liability side, which suggests that it
is mostly hedging rather than speculative exposure.
On the liabilities side, total external liabilities increased from USD 7.919 trillion to US
D 11.906 trillion in 2024, with this growth being faster than asset growth and thus directly exp
laining the deepenin of the negative NIIP throughout the period. The portfolio of bond liabilit
ies is convincing dominant item: increased from USD 2.461 trillion in 2010 to USD 3.262 trillion in 2024, whic
h directly reflects the foreign share of ownership of French government bonds (OAT) and corp
orate debt instruments. According to the Banque de France, the share of foreign investors in t
he total debt of the central sector fluctuated between 52% and 55.8% (2024) throughout the an
alyzed period. This concentration of foreign ownership of government debt is France's basic st
ructural vulnerability, as it makes borrowing costs sensitive to changes in global risk appetite.
Other investments in liabilities increased from 2.502 to 3.764 trillion USD and, togethe
r with assets in this category, reflect interbank exposure. According to the Banque de France r
eport for 2024, the banking sector has a structural NIIP deficit that amounted to -EUR 502 bill
ion in 2024, with the fact that it showed an improvement of EUR 102 billion compared to the
previous year. FDI liabilities increased from 1.015 to 1.494 trillion USD, but remain lower tha
n FDI assets - which means that France has a positive net FDI position that brings income fro
m direct investments abroad (net FDI income is positive even in a crisis).
According to the Banque de France for 2024, the net FDI position amounts to +EUR 5
68 billion by book value, and about +EUR 811 billion by market value, because the book valu
e of unlisted companies systematically underestimates the market value.

### 4. Degree of financial globalization

4.1 Indicator (A+L)/GDP

The EWN methodology uses a central indicator of financial globalization. The indicat
or is the ratio of total external claims and liabilities to nominal GDP: (Total assets + Total liab
ilities) / GDP. The indicator shows the depth of connection of the national economy with glob
al money flows and the strength of external connections. For France, the indicator rose from 5.85 times gross domestic product in 2010 to 7.97 t
imes gross domestic product in 2020 – the highest result in the observed period – and then slig
htly decreased to 7.25 times gross domestic product in 2024. This level of financial globalizat
ion places France at the very top of the most integrated economy, along with Luxembourg, Irel
and, the Netherlands and Great Britain. France is far ahead of typical developed economies, w
hose ratio ranges between 2 and 4 times the gross domestic product.
This great openness has a historical reason: Paris is one of the five largest financial cen
ters in the world. French institutional investors – insurance companies, pension funds, UCITS
funds – are constantly investing money everywhere. French multinationals – Total Energies, L
VMH, AXA, Airbus – are also investing globally. The paradox of dirigisme is this: a state tha
t interferes in the country is very open and connected to the whole world outside.

4.2 The banking sector as a transmission mechanism of globalization

The enormous balance of the sum of four systemically important banks, Global System
ically Important Banks, raises the (A+L)/Gross domestic product indicator. BNP Paribas has t
otal assets that have repeatedly exceeded 2.5 trillion euros in that period. BNP Paribas' balance
sheet exceeds the annual Gross Domestic Product of France. Société Générale and Crédit Agr
icole each add 1.5–1.7 trillion euros. BPCE adds about 1.2–1.3 trillion euro (Financial Stabilit
y Board, 2024). An insight into these aggregate values confirms the hypothesis of a high conc
entration of economic power.
Institutions work as "too big to fail" in two ways. First, the institutions are too big for t
he French banking system. Second, the institutions are too large for the European regulatory fr
amework. Any shock that hits one of these institutions must ask for help. The Banque de Fran
ce and the Autorité de contrôle prudentiel et de résolution, the European Stability Mechanism
and possibly the European Central Bank are seeking help. This importance of institutions is al
so a strength because the state guarantee gives an additional plus in the credit rating, and the w
eakness occurs in risk taking. The increase in the (A+L)/GDP indicator in 2020 (from 6.31x t
o 7.97x) comes mostly from a lot of bank lending. Lending was through the ECB TLTRO-III
program and state guarantees for loans during the period COVID-19 crisis. Crédit Agricole used more than EUR 50 billion through TLTRO operation
s. This directly increased the balance sheet and thus the external financial stock.

4.3 Index reduction 2022–2024: Cyclical correction or structural trend?

Later, the (A+L)/GDP ratio slightly decreased from 7.97x (2020) to 7.25x (2024). We
propose a partial de-globalization of the balance sheet - deleveraging triggered by higher ECB
interest rates from 2022 to 2024. The growth of reference rates from -0.5 % to +4.0 % in just 1
4 months caused a repricing of external positions, a decrease in appetite for cross-border portf
olio investments and compression of derivative positions with banks. The banks seem to be un
der pressure. Absolute levels of assets and liabilities continue to rise (11+ trillion USD on eac
h side in 2024). This shows that it is a relative – and not an absolute – correction. The relative
correction partly reflects the growth of nominal GDP (the denominator in the ratio) through inflation.

### 5. Conclusion – SWOT analysis of the financial position of France

Analysis of France in the period 2010–2024. through the prism of EWN data and IMF
methodology, it reveals tension: France is an economy that shows external vulnerability and h
as financial instruments. The following SWOT analysis synthesizes the findings from the pre
vious parts and projects them to the next medium-term period (2025–2029).
Strength factors show that France is still a financially independent country with global
power: (1) The Euro as a reserve currency eliminates the "original son" problem. France does
not take debts in foreign currency. There is no currency risk that could trigger a 1997 crisis in
Asia; (2) Gold reserves of 2,437 tons (204 billion US dollars in 2024) provide the best buffer
that does not depend on the confidence of foreign creditors; (3) The (A+L)/Gross Domestic Pr
oduct ratio appears to be 7.25 times the Gross Domestic Product and shows that the economy i
s highly connected to global financial flows. This is why the "flight to quality" crisis from Fra
nce cannot happen - a systemic shock would hit the entire eurozone at once; (4) The national
champions of the banking sector – BNP Paribas, Crédit Agricole, Société Générale and BPCE
– guarantee loans, liquidity and international payments thanks to implicit state protection. In a
strategically relevant threat from the fragmentation of the Eurozone, France could be a parado
xical winner through the diversion of capital towards safe OAT bonds and due to its high credi
tworthiness (AA/Aa2). On the vulnerability side, the following factors dominate: NIIP without gold reserves r
eached -29.7 % of GDP in 2024 (compared to -12.6 % in 2010) according to the Lane & Miles
i-Ferretti basis, with a net external debt of 42% of GDP. This means that foreign actors have a
ccumulated net claims against France, almost three trillion dollars (USD). The current account
was in the red 12 of the 15 years we reviewed. The overall minus shows that the negative net i
nternational investment position is accumulating. Public debt of 112% of GDP is largely finan
ced by foreign investors – 55.8% of government bonds are foreign-owned according to the Ba
nque de France – making borrowing costs vulnerable to confidence shocks. A high Credit-to-
GDP ratio (>220%) and an over-concentrated banking sector increase systemic risk. In the ev
ent of a strong credit shock hitting any of the four G-SIB banks, the cost of bailing out the gov
ernment would be enormous. the high public debt amounts to 112 % of the gross domestic pro
duct. Foreign investors they finance. This makes France more sensitive to global shocks and changes in market sentim
ent. Dirigisme and the neo-Keynesian model of public finances that France applies represe
nt a systemic choice. This choice means that the accumulation of external liabilities is accepte
d and that a negative NIIP is tolerated. This is paid for by high social protection, active industr
ial policy and stability of the financial sector. The alternative is structural reforms according t
o the Anglo-Saxon model. These reforms would reduce public expenditure and reduce debt an
d could fix the NIIP. But this would cost social instability and could break the model that prov
ides long-term political and social stability. The final assessment says that France is not in fin
ancial trouble in 2024, but the external position requires attention. If the bad trend of NIIP con
tinues and we do not fix the current account, the total cost of servicing net external liabilities
will increasingly burden the fiscal space and economic stability. The risk increases if France l
oses its AAA/AA+ rating, as this will raise the cost of borrowing. High public debt can trigger
a refinancing spiral, as Italy experienced in 2011–2012. This is why the Banque de France and
the ECB constantly monitor the NIIP, as the main sign of system stability.
