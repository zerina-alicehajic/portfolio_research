### Abstract
This essay argues that the critical minerals crisis stems from Western economic strategies rather than geological factors or Chinese actions. Drawing on the classical liberal tradition, the essay demonstrates that China's dominance in mineral processing is a predictable outcome of comparative advantage, shaped by Western regulatory burdens and market distortions. The author critiques US and EU policies, including "friend-shoring" and bilateral agreements, as further distortions that fail to address supply vulnerabilities. The essay introduces "referential systems of power" to show that geopolitical competition centers on processing patents and trade standards rather than mining rights. It concludes that Western governments have fostered the dependency they now seek to mitigate and that proposed solutions may entrench the issue further. It also examines how the BRICS+6 bloc, leveraging its collective mineral endowments, is constructing an alternative governance framework that directly challenges the MSP's claim to set the terms of global critical mineral trade. 

* **Keywords:** critical minerals, comparative advantage, strategic autonomy, friend-shoring, Minerals Security Partnership, regulatory capture, standard-setting power, economic diplomacy

### 1. Introduction: The Manufactured Scarcity

The criticality of a raw material is generally defined by two dimensions: the material's economic importance and the risk of a supply disruption. While economic importance is often measured by the value added by sectors that use the material, supply risk is linked to the concentration of production in countries with low political stability. Pehlken et al. (2015) emphasize that "criticality is a dynamic, technology-dependent concept." The Critical Raw Materials Act (CRM Act) defines the list using "a methodology that assesses the economic importance for EU industry and the thresholds of risks in supply caused by high concentrations in third countries that do not share the same environmental and social standards."

Despite their name, Rare Earth Elements (REE) are relatively abundant in the Earth's crust. Their "rarity" stems from the difficulty of finding them in concentrations that are economically viable to extract and process without causing catastrophic environmental damage (Siddiqui, 2025). Frenzel et al. (2017) note that criticality is not a measure of geological exhaustion but rather of technical and political bottlenecks in the supply chain. IEA (2021) shows that Lithium is not geologically scarce. However, the lead time to bring new projects from discovery to production averages 16.5 years, creating a structural lag that defines their criticality more than their crustal abundance.

The real geopolitical power lies in the refining stage. Even though minerals like lithium and cobalt are mined in Australia or the DRC (upstream), they are mostly shipped to China for chemical processing (downstream) into battery-grade materials (Zhang, 2025). China has focused on the downstream sector for decades, knowing that adding value through processing makes countries more economically dependent than simply extracting raw materials (Kalantzakos, 2020). Siddiqui (2025) says that while mining (upstream) is spread out geographically, China's dominance is most clear in the midstream and downstream segments. China controls almost 87% of the world's capacity for separating and refining rare earths.

When examining the drivers of demand, the most common factors are electric cars and renewables. UNCTAD (2020) shows that the digital and green "twin transition" is creating a paradigm shift in material intensity. By 2050, the demand for minerals such as graphite, lithium, and cobalt could increase by almost 500% to meet the needs of clean energy technologies. The transition from a fuel-intensive energy system (fossil fuels) to a material-intensive system (renewables) fundamentally changes the definition of energy security. For example, a typical electric car needs six times as many minerals as a regular car, and an offshore wind plant needs nine times as many minerals as a gas-fired plant.

Unlike oil, a flow resource that can be cut off to halt an economy immediately, minerals are stock resources. Once a battery is built, it provides energy for years. Therefore, mineral "embargoes" have a different temporal impact than oil shocks (Øverland, 2018). The new mineral economy is characterized by a "new mercantilism." While global markets and OPEC governed oil, critical minerals are increasingly governed by bilateral "friend-shoring" agreements and industrial policy (Humphreys, 2018). The geography of oil is fixed by nature; the geography of mineral processing is fixed by industrial investment and state strategy, making it a more "controllable" geopolitical tool (Vivoda, Matthews, & McGregor, 2023).

### 2. Comparative Advantage and the Anatomy of Self-Inflicted Dependency

Ricardo's principle of comparative advantage is among the most empirically robust propositions in economics and among the most politically ignored. A country should export what it has a relative advantage in and import others, facilitating mutual gains even if one country has an absolute disadvantage in all goods. China's dominance in mineral processing is a textbook example. The longstanding regulatory framework, adjusted for industrial expansion rather than to satisfy European Green Party MPs, allows China to have lower environmental compliance costs. With lower labor costs, cheaper energy in processing-intensive inland provinces, and high levels of accumulated human capital in hydrometallurgy (Siddiqui, 2025), combined with solvent extraction, it cannot be replaced that quickly by just hiring an outside mining company.

On the other hand, Western nations have purposely destroyed their own processing industries with tough environmental regulation, people protesting government decisions, and following the logic of importing clean while exporting dirty industries. This shift represents a textbook case of the Pollution Haven Hypothesis (PHH), where stringent domestic regulations in the West led to the offshoring of "dirty" industries to jurisdictions with more flexible frameworks (Cole, 2004). The late-1990s Mountain Pass closure, driven by regulatory pressures, forced US processing to relocate to China, rendering domestic operations unviable (Nakano, 2021). Now, some might think a conspiracy is at play here, with China making strategic moves, but it is essentially just the market working exactly as it should.

Amid intense pressure and lobbying through the European Green Deal and the U.S. Inflation Reduction Act, we now ponder what the "green transition" has added to this distorted picture. Policies such as lowering carbon emissions and commitments to net-zero timelines created a mandated demand shock for those minerals in Chinese supply chains. No actual policies were in place in the EU or in America to address their own shortages, which focused on expanding capabilities on their own soil. As a result, Chinese firms were building battery gigafactories in Hungary and Morocco. At the same time, the European Commission spent 3 years drafting regulations for a dozen critical raw materials it had identified (European Parliament & Council, 2024). China has done nothing wrong here. In simpler terms, Western policy has created a structural supply-demand mismatch through interventionist frameworks. This crisis is government-manufactured, and responding to it with more intervention is an overbearing conceit.

### 3. "Strategic Autonomy" and the Protectionist Reflex

What sounds serious in phrasing but serves as a political placebo is the oft-used "strategic autonomy." Its use has been known in industrial policy, trade law, and now mineral economics. We have heard it in speeches by former French President Macron, who referenced the EU defense system and national security interests. The hidden meaning behind it is simple protectionism: producing domestically what countries currently import, regardless of cost. However the EU wants to frame it, it will not change its welfare consequences.

The institutional context for strategic autonomy in the critical minerals domain is governed by the Minerals Security Partnership (MSP), launched in June 2022 under U.S. leadership and encompassing Australia, Canada, the EU, South Korea, the United Kingdom, and others. Vivoda and colleagues (2024) have described it as a mechanism to "friend-shore" critical mineral supply chains, which means reorienting sourcing toward those geopolitical allies and far away from strategic adversaries, particularly China. What seems simple and reasonable is preferential trade agreements with geopolitical allies who share the West's regulatory standards, ESG frameworks, and labor norms.

One question that remains unanswered concerns the costs of friend-shoring. The true costs are unknown because no welfare analysis is presented. If cobalt were processed in Quebec rather than in Zhejiang, one would expect it to require ongoing bailouts, and additional financial costs would arise from the potential redirection of trade flows. These include training the labor force, building processing infrastructure, and accepting environmental compliance costs. According to the IEA, meeting net-zero targets by 2050 would require a 4- to 6-fold increase in the supply of critical minerals (IEA, 2021). Friend-shoring will not suffice, so the only option left is to raise the marginal cost of the energy transition while transferring rents from consumers to politically connected mining and processing companies in OECD jurisdictions.

The deeply rooted problem of friend-shoring contradicts the foundation of comparative advantage, from which we derive our welfare-enhancing benefits from international trade (Frenzel, Kullik, Reuter, & Gutzmer, 2017). For example, Siddiqui (2025) mentions Zambia as a cheaper alternative to Canada for cobalt production. By that logic, we should invest more and focus on Zambia, regardless of "friend" status. MSP would say this is a security concern and, as such, cannot be taken into real consideration, so it overrides efficiency. This makes strategic autonomy a political maneuver that prevents any real economic analysis from yielding results that do not favor the allies. Once more, the problems lie in the West's dependence on Chinese processing, stemming from its own poor policy choices. Giuli & Oberthür (2023) analyze the decision to subsidize through the CMA, the IRA, and other bilateral agreements. This requires constant increases in public expenditure to stay afloat and not be buried under the dominance of the Chinese, who, as a result of the agreement, benefit even more from state support. So, to solve this subsidy-created problem, we get more subsidies. This is a strategy implemented in a sector in which our main competitor has more fiscal space and a 40-year head start. In reality, this is a form of economic denial.

### 4. Referential Systems of Power: Who Controls the Standards?

A broader understanding of geopolitics can be gained through analysis of the referential system, including control and pricing, certification, trading, and consumption. What can be called the "referential power system" in the international political economy of minerals is the institutional architecture—technical standards, certifications, and price benchmarks—that determines whose rules govern the market (Vivoda, Matthews, & McGregor, 2023; Zhang, 2025). As Siddiqui (2025) points out, control over these processes (processing capacity) allows China to dictate the terms of dependency, while the US and the EU attempt to establish their own "clubs" (MSPs) to redefine these standards (Zhou & Månberger, 2024). To sum up, the country or bloc that has control and power does not need to own a mine; it only needs the standard.

One of the world's financial centers, the UK, more specifically, is home to the London Metal Exchange (LME), which sets benchmark prices for copper, nickel, aluminum, and other metals. This is the definition of referential authority. When a deal is made between a cobalt mine in DRC and a battery manufacturer in South Korea, the price is indexed to a benchmark that reflects the institutional preferences of a Western-led trading system. Kalantzakos (2020) writes that capital flows are in the hands of the most powerful economies, marking this as the second financial globalization. To challenge this, China created the Shanghai Futures Exchange and its international board. Also, the Belt and Road Initiative is another referential system with Chinese infrastructure financing, processing technology, and pricing benchmarks. All steps are taken to differentiate and offer a second choice.

The Western economies have not stayed silent on this. Their response is more focused on securing mine sites, negotiating agreements, and building the necessary processing facilities. These are real, concrete measures, but no strategic decisions have been made regarding the critical issues of Chinese dominance in patent industrial policy and technology acquisitions. Specifically, China has a set of processing patents and technical know-how in battery chemistry that far surpasses Western standards. This gives China the referential authority over the quality standards for the critical mineral supply (Zhang, 2025), so when a battery cell is produced in Western economies, the standard testing for performance, durability, and safety will be conducted against the standards originally developed in China's industrial practice.

According to Humphreys (2019), structural price volatility, once controlled by long-term contract pricing, has been driven by the shift toward spot pricing in commodity markets, partly due to Asian, especially Chinese, market practices. This is not merely an economic scenario; it reflects the growing ability of Chinese actors to shape a time-based framework for commodity markets. The influence of the power shift stemming from specific pricing conventions leading to referential authority is more important than any bilateral agreement.

Therefore, now we shall ask ourselves what a market-liberal approach to referential power entails. The answer is structured in two parts. On the one hand, it involves preserving and developing the Western institutional infrastructure, namely the LME, international accounting and contract standards, and commodity certification. Regarding the discussion on subsidies, a critical point emerges. None of the above policies requires government subsidies; the government itself needs to play a minimalist role and focus on preventing regulatory fragmentation that makes standards less attractive to producers in developing countries. On the other side, greater investment in intellectual property is needed, which will then create the technical reference authority through increased R&D in collaboration with universities, private R&D initiatives, and the development of competitive patent systems. This also does not need a Minerals Security Partnership. Essential to the second part is the strong willpower to empower the private sector and its initiatives.

### 5. The War of Standards: MSP vs. BRICS+6 and the Future of Mineral Governance

The literature often refers to a 'resource war' when addressing the complex geopolitical contest over critical minerals. The problem with this framing is that disputes arise not over minerals themselves, but over standards. The outlook for 2030 and beyond will bring heightened competition regarding technical specifications, governance frameworks, and pricing conventions. In this section, I will explain the divergence between MSP and the BRICS bloc.

The MSP, as Vivoda et al. (2024) document, is explicitly structured around what it calls "high environmental, social, and governance (ESG) standards." Under ESG standards, Western economies automatically exclude partnerships with developing countries that do not comply, meaning that a mine in Katanga that cannot afford a Deloitte ESG certification will not be in the Western supply chain. Simply put, it is a non-tariff barrier that systematically disadvantages producing countries that are unable to effectively implement policies, manage resources, and comply with the audit requirements designed for OECD jurisdictions. This includes independent audit, community consultation, environmental impact assessment, and labor standards consistent with OECD norms. This exclusion comes from the compliance architecture that merges governance capacity with governance quality.

Here, BRICS emerges. China seems to be a better alternative, at least offering a transactional agreement that bypasses ESG certification, provides infrastructure investment without governance conditions, and facilitates processing partnerships without requiring institutional reform. What could be described as a "race to the bottom" from the perspective of strict Western governments is, for the sovereign countries in the BRICS, a calculated risk that better serves their development goals. One might think that developing countries will prioritize Western institutional preferences over their own economic interests. However, that assumption is flawed unless they are competitive on price and have market access.

The BRICS expanded in January 2024, adding Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Ethiopia, Iran, Egypt, and Argentina to the original five (Khodary, Selmey, & Elsayed, 2024; Mirović & Petrović, 2023, p. 11). Müller (2025) notes that with this expansion, the bloc controls a significant share of the world's critical mineral endowments. Argentina, through a bilateral agreement with the DRC, has vast lithium and cobalt reserves. Ethiopia has enormous mining potential, and the UAE provides much-needed capital to fund BRICS mineral projects, independent of Western influence in capital markets. In this arrangement, the BRICS countries supply the necessary minerals as an independent governance system. At the same time, China operates refineries and processing facilities to convert raw materials into the commodities needed by the global economy.

The rules-based international trading system (RBITS), centered on the WTO, is the framework for global trade, grounded in common rules that make international exchanges open, predictable, and inclusive (Varnavskii, 2020, p. 106). One of its tasks was to prevent this bifurcation, but it failed to do so (Escaith, 2022, p. 415). The WTO's authority is being undermined by the growth of plurilateral agreements that avoid multilateral disciplines and by the paralysis of its Appellate Body, largely due to U.S. obstruction under both the Trump and Biden administrations (Mukhopadhyay, 2019; Howse, 2021, p. 103). The MSP is a plurilateral arrangement operating outside the WTO framework and is seen as a preferential trading agreement that discriminates against non-members (Roy & Marchetti, 2014, p. 709). The irony is that the U.S. and EU have lectured the world on nondiscriminatory practices for three decades, yet now use the same practices in a sector vital to the economy (Brunat & Fontanel, 2024, p. 13).

The classical liberal perspective teaches us that Western countries have lost their most powerful instrument for maintaining normative authority over the global economy (Schwarzer, 2017). Although they are still improving technical standards, they have abandoned the rules-based international trading system (Weithmann & Luedtke, 2023). After all, the WTO made China operate under strict rules (Lawrence, 2006, p. 8), but the MSP allows China to maneuver around them and is free to ignore them completely. The new BRICS arrangements make that possible (Kamin & Langhammer, 2023, p. 264). The moves Western governments made were, simply put, ignorant and naive. How can one win the standards war if you withdraw from the standards forum?

### 6. What a Market-Liberal Policy Would Actually Look Like

While I enjoy critiquing the policy framework, I find it difficult to specify an alternative. Upon reviewing the existing literature, it is evident that critics of the market-liberal position hold a consensus that "the market will not solve security problems" (Loader & White, 2015, p. 174). This claim is true to an extent, but it is also somewhat irrelevant, since the security concerns they fear stem from market distortions. If we think we can remove these concerns by adding new institutions, subsidy programs, or entering into new partnerships, it would not solve anything. Contrary to this belief, the true answer lies in having the courage to remove existing ones.

The market-liberal reform agenda has five components. First, reforming the existing system, starting with permits. Approving a new mineral development project in the US typically takes 16 to 25 years; Australia and Canada are close behind, with 7-10 and 8-15 years, respectively (IEA, 2021). The timeline and the reality of environmental complexity are not in sync. This only benefits those litigation-industrial actors. Corporate actors themselves often use litigation strategically as a "private generalizable interest" to impact regulatory environments. Establishing statutory regulatory deadlines, limiting third-party legal standing to those with a proven material interest, and making a clear distinction between environmental assessments (which are acceptable) and interminable administrative obstruction (which are not) are all aspects of a market-liberal approach.

The second covers the importance of the price signal. Within three decades, the IEA's Net Zero by 2050 scenario calls for 25 times current cobalt production and 40 times current lithium production. This is a price signal, not a crisis. Overland (2019) describes that the projected demand will rise, and so will the prices of critical minerals, making previously deemed economically unviable deposits viable. The money will be used to fund R&D on material substitution and to attract capital back into recycling infrastructure. This is an essential process to solve scarcity problems. What stands in the way of a smooth process are market distortions on the demand side, which distort the temporal price signal, and on the supply side, preventing the proper investment response. If we were to remove the distortions, comparative advantage would then allocate production efficiently.

Third, invest in human capital and intellectual property. Realistically, only the most advanced economic powers can do so, and one cannot simply buy power by acquiring a mine or refinery in another country. This will give you an opportunity, but it will not create reference authority on its own. There is a need to improve a patent portfolio in battery chemistry, to maintain a standard-setting presence in the ISO and IEC technical committees that write the specifications for electric vehicle components, and to build a university research ecosystem that produces the next generation of hydrometallurgical engineers. With this in mind, the government and nations can expect funds and support from markets, since it will generate higher returns. This is a better option than simply building refineries, since a patent portfolio has substantial market value independent of political support.

The most counterproductive move in Western trade strategy over the past ten years has been the United States deliberate decision to paralyze the WTO Appellate Body. This decision has persisted across administrations and is bipartisan in its indifference. Collaborating with the WTO Appellate Body gives Western economies an advantage. It can be a useful asset for constraining Chinese export restrictions on critical minerals, better than any bilateral agreement could be. Trade has rules established at an incredible diplomatic cost, so willingly choosing to ignore this relationship might yield, if any, short-term gains but also triggers what (Friedman & Friedman, 1980; Friedman M., 1962) would call political, rather than economic, thinking.

The fifth step will be difficult for Western economies. One must accept that China's involvement in processing is, to a degree, economically rational and will continue regardless of policy. No one can take away China's comparative advantages in mineral processing, so the goal is not to work harder to destabilize their position but to focus on upping your own game. Western firms and governments have to innovate, develop higher technical standards, diversify sourcing relationships, and build institutional leverage to prevent monopolistic tendencies by any actors in the international market. The controlling narrative focuses on state subsidies and guarantees failure; the second creates an opportunity to balance market conditions and support private investment in processing diversity. Not focusing on barriers, and definitely not adding any more procurement requirements.

### 7. Conclusion

The geopolitics of critical minerals is a story about what happens when a generation of policymakers mistakes the expression of preferences for the management of consequences. Western governments expressed a preference for a green economy. They did not manage the consequence that such an economy requires inputs whose production they, by their own regulatory choices, had made increasingly unviable at home. They expressed a preference for clean supply chains. They did not manage the consequence that enforcing ESG standards on mineral producers drives those producers toward Chinese financing, which imposes no such conditions. They expressed a preference for supply chain security. They did not manage the consequence that building that security through preferential trade arrangements destroys the multilateral framework that was Western leverage's institutional foundation, most useful insight (Friedman & Friedman, 1980; Friedman M., 1962). It was about the pervasive tendency of government intervention to produce outcomes opposite to its stated intentions, because intervention operates on visible, immediate effects. In contrast, markets operate on dispersed, long-term incentives. The MSP is an attempt to solve a mineral concentration problem with industrial policy. It will produce a more expensive mineral supply chain, a more fragmented multilateral trading system, and, after the subsidy cycle has run its course, a supply chain that is only marginally less concentrated in Chinese processing, because the underlying cost advantages have not been addressed.

The "war of standards" that defines the real geopolitical contest will not be won by building refineries with government money. It will be won or lost in the technical committees of ISO, the patent offices of Munich and Washington, the research laboratories of Zurich and Cambridge, and the contract law chambers where commodity transactions are structured and adjudicated. These are domains of private initiative and intellectual excellence. They are not domains that respond well to political management. The choice, ultimately, is between a market-liberal approach that maintains Western referential authority through institutional and intellectual excellence and an interventionist approach that seeks to replicate Chinese industrial policy within the inefficiencies of Western institutions. The first is difficult. The second is expensive and almost certainly futile.
